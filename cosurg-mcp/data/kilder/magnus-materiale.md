# 1. Introduction to Burns

Burn injuries are a common form of trauma that affect the skin and underlying tissues due to exposure to heat, chemicals, electricity, radiation, or friction. Burns can range from minor injuries requiring minimal care to life-threatening conditions needing advanced medical and surgical treatment.

Burns are not only painful and potentially disfiguring, but they also provoke a systemic inflammatory and metabolic response, especially in patients with large total body surface area (TBSA) involvement. Severe burns can lead to hypovolemia, infection, and multi-organ failure if not treated appropriately.

Children and the elderly are particularly vulnerable populations due to thinner skin and reduced physiological reserves.

Burn care involves multidisciplinary management, from initial resuscitation and wound care to rehabilitation and scar management. A basic understanding of burn classification, pathophysiology, and initial treatment principles is essential for any healthcare professional involved in acute or long-term care of burn patients.

# 2. Anatomy of the Skin

Understanding the anatomy of the skin is essential for assessing burn depth and guiding treatment. The skin is the body's largest organ and serves as a barrier against environmental harm, prevents fluid loss, regulates temperature, and plays a role in immune defense.

## Layers of the Skin:

### Epidermis

* The outermost layer
* Composed primarily of keratinocytes
* Provides waterproofing and protection
* Contains no blood vessels
* Lines the hair follicles, sweat glands, and sebaceous glands (collectively referred to as skin adnexal structures)

### Dermis

* Located beneath the epidermis
* Rich in collagen and elastin fibers
* Contains blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles, sweat glands, and sebaceous glands
* Responsible for skin strength, sensation, and thermoregulation

### Subcutaneous Tissue (Hypodermis)

* Fat and connective tissue layer beneath the dermis
* Provides insulation, cushioning, and energy storage

# 3. Pathophysiology of Burns

Burn injuries initiate a complex series of local and systemic physiological responses. The extent of these changes depends on the depth of the burn, the total body surface area (TBSA) involved, and whether inhalation injury is present. The damage of burns should be evaluated both locally and systemically.

## Local Response

Local tissue injury in burns can be described using Jackson's burn wound model (1947), consisting of three concentric zones:

### Zone of Coagulation (Zone 1)

* + Caused by direct heat exposure. Tissue necrosis and irreversible protein denaturation occur here. This is the point of maximum damage.

### Zone of Stasis (Zone 2)

* + Surrounds the central necrotic zone. Blood flow is reduced, and the tissue has approximately a 50% chance of survival. Proper early management, such as adequate resuscitation and cooling, can preserve tissue. However, further damage due to infection, edema, or hypotension can convert this zone to necrosis.

### Zone of Hyperemia (Zone 3)

* + The outermost zone with increased perfusion and inflammation. Tissue in this area generally recovers unless compromised by sepsis or prolonged hypoperfusion.

## Systemic Response

(typically with >20% TBSA burns in adults or >10% in children)

When tissue is damaged by a burn injury, it triggers an immune response involving the release of inflammatory mediators such as histamine, prostaglandins, and cytokines. In smaller burns, this response remains local. However, in large burns, it becomes systemic, affecting respiratory and cardiovascular systems, metabolic rate, and immune function.

This widespread inflammation alters homeostasis, fluid balance, and nutrient absorption, and induces a hypermetabolic state. Immunosuppression occurs, increasing the risk of infection.
Capillary permeability increases, causing plasma to leak into surrounding tissues. Vasodilation—mediated in part by nitric oxide synthase (NOS)—reduces vascular resistance, contributing to intravascular volume depletion.

At the same time, regional vasoconstriction occurs, especially in the splanchnic and peripheral circulation, as a compensatory response to maintain perfusion to vital organs. This imbalance can further compromise tissue oxygenation in non-central organs.

These combined changes lead to a catabolic state, hypovolemia, and a risk of multi-organ failure.

Additionally, patients with large burns often experience significant heat loss. The skin serves as a critical barrier for thermoregulation, and when its integrity is compromised, especially over large surface areas, the body is unable to retain heat. As a result, burn patients are at high risk of hypothermia, particularly during initial resuscitation and wound care. Hypothermia may further impair immune function, coagulation, and tissue perfusion, and must be actively prevented by maintaining a warm environment and using external heat sources

It is also common to observe febrile episodes, especially between day 2 and 5 post-injury, most notably in children. These fever peaks are often driven by the immune system’s response to tissue injury rather than infection, and they reflect systemic inflammation. They do not in themselves indicate a need for antibiotic treatment but should prompt careful observation, as persistent fever may lead to dehydration and organ strain, which could necessitate supportive care.

## Burn Shock

Burn shock is a specific form of hypovolemic shock that typically develops within the first 24 hours after major burns. It results from profound intravascular fluid loss due to the capillary leakage and vasodilation.

Reduced circulating volume impairs cardiac output and oxygen delivery to vital organs. If not promptly treated with fluid resuscitation, this can progress to tissue hypoperfusion, hypoxia, and multi-organ failure.

Fluid resuscitation is essential to restore effective circulation. Urine output is commonly used as a clinical marker for the adequacy of resuscitation.

**Key features of burn shock:**

* Hypovolemia
* Hypotension
* Tachycardia
* Oliguria
* Cold, clammy extremities

# Circular Burns

In deep dermal or full-thickness burns, the burned skin loses its elasticity and cannot expand with the underlying tissue swelling.
Burns encircling limbs, thorax, or abdomen can then lead to vascular or ventilatory compromise.x

## Limb burns:

Circumferential burns on the extremities can lead to increased pressure within the affected limb due to swelling and the formation of a rigid eschar. This creates a constrictive effect, similar to a tourniquet, that may compress blood vessels and nerves, compromising distal perfusion. Without timely intervention, this can progress to ischemia, nerve injury, and ultimaly limb loss.

### **Symptoms:**

These symptoms are typically observed distal to the circumferential burn site and reflect compromised perfusion due to increased pressure within the limb. They are collectively referred to as the "5 P's," which are classic signs of reduced peripheral circulation:

* Pallor (pale or bluish discoloration distal to the burn)
* Pain (especially increasing with movement)
* Paresthesia (tingling or numbness)
* Pulselessness (diminished or absent distal pulses)
* Paralysis (reduced or absent movement)

### **Clinical examination:**

* Assessment of skin color and temperature
* Capillary refill and pulse palpation
* Use of pulse oximetry on affected fingers or toes

### **Treatment:**

* Surgical escharotomy may be necessary to relieve pressure and restore circulation

## Thoracic burns:

Deep dermal or full-thickness circumferential burns of the chest can impair chest wall expansion, compromising ventilation.

### **Signs:**

* Decreased chest wall movement
* Increased work of breathing
* Hypoxia or respiratory distress
* Often, the patient is intubated due to respiratory compromise, and increased resistance may be observed during mechanical ventilation

### **Treatment:**

* Urgent escharotomy to restore chest wall compliance and improve ventilation

## Abdominal burns:

Circumferential abdominal burns can increase intra-abdominal pressure and lead to abdominal compartment syndrome. This condition reduces venous return, impairs renal perfusion, and compromises ventilation, potentially resulting in multi-organ dysfunction.

In children, anterior abdominal burns alone can impair diaphragmatic excursion due to tight eschar formation, leading to respiratory distress.

# Escharotomy

In patients with deep circumferential burns of the extremities, thorax, abdomen, or neck, escharotomy is a surgical procedure used to relieve pressure caused by the rigid, non-elastic burn eschar. This pressure can impair circulation or ventilation and lead to serious complications such as ischemia, respiratory compromise, or abdominal compartment syndrome.

## **Procedure:**

* Performed by making linear incisions through the burned skin (eschar) down to the subcutaneous fat
* Incisions must begin in unburnt skin, cross the entire thickness of the burn, and extend into unburnt tissue on the opposite side
* Only the skin should be incised—fascial compartments must not be entered during escharotomy (unlike fasciotomy)
* May be performed at bedside or in the operating room depending on urgency and setting
* Common incision sites:
 + **Upper limbs:** Mid-medial and mid-lateral lines
 + **Lower limbs:** Mid-medial and mid-lateral lines
 + **Thorax:** Along the anterior axillary lines (may be connected across the front at the upper thorax just beneath the clavicles and under the costa margin og the upper abdomen.
 + **Neck:** Vertical incisions along in the midline to relieve airway compression

This procedure restores perfusion in compromised extremities and improves ventilation when thoracic or cervical expansion is impaired.

### Receiving a Burn in a Trauma Center / Emergency Room

The majority of burns can be safely managed in local emergency departments or outpatient clinics, particularly if they are small and superficial. Burns can broadly be divided into those suitable for local management and those requiring referral to a specialized burn center.

## **Should be referred to a burn center:**

* Burn injury exceeds 5% of TBSA in children (<15 years) and exceeds 10% of TBSA in adults
* Pregnant, elderly, and infants, as well as large burns on hands, feet, face, and genitalia, should be considered for hospitalization
* Subdermal burns exceeding 5% of TBSA
* If escharotomies are indicated
* Electrical or chemical injuries
* Suspected or diagnosed inhalation injury
* If there is any doubt regarding treatment

These criteria help ensure patients receive appropriate care in specialized settings.

## **Management of severe burns**

Initial management of a severe burn patient in the trauma center or emergency department follows the principles of Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) and includes a structured ABCDE approach to ensure rapid stabilization. This structured sequence is essential, as burn patients may present with additional injuries or complications (e.g., blunt trauma, inhalation injuries, explosions). ABCDE ensures that life-threatening conditions—such as airway obstruction, respiratory failure, or circulatory collapse—are identified and treated first.

### Primary Survey – ABCDE

* **Airway with cervical spine protection**
 + Assess for inhalation injury (hoarseness, stridor, burns in the mouth or nose, soot in sputum)
 + Be prepared for early intubation in patients with signs of airway compromise
* **Breathing**
 + Assess chest wall movement
 + Circumferential thoracic burns may restrict ventilation and require escharotomy
* **Circulation**
 + Assess for signs of burn shock (cool extremities, delayed capillary refill, tachycardia)
 + Establish IV access (preferably two large-bore peripheral lines in unburned skin)
* **Disability (neurologic status)**
 + Brief neurological exam (AVPU)
* **Exposure and environmental control**
 + Completely undress the patient and assess for other trauma
 + Prevent hypothermia (cover with clean sheets, warm fluids, warm ambient temperature)
 + Estimate the burn, depth and size

### FATT – Fluids, Analgesia, Tests, Tubes

Following the primary survey, the FATT approach should be initiated to stabilize the patient and prepare for further management.

* **Fluids**: Start fluid resuscitation in patients with >10% TBSA burns in children or >20% in adults, typically using the Parkland formula.
* **Analgesia**: Administer adequate intravenous pain relief—opioids are often required.
* **Tests**: Take relevant blood tests including hemoglobin, electrolytes, glucose, carboxyhemoglobin, lactate, and urinalysis (especially in electrical injuries).
* **Tubes**: Insert a urinary catheter to monitor urine output, and nasogastric tube placement in large burns or in cases with potential gastric dilation or ileus.

This step ensures that physiological support is initiated early and helps guide continued management.

### First Aid

Ensure that any clothing or jewelry is removed carefully, especially rings and watches which may impair circulation with swelling

If the burn has not been cooled immediately after the injury, it is still beneficial to apply cool running water (approx. 15 degree celcius) for up to 20 minutes—as long as it is within 3 hours of the injury. This helps reduce the depth and severity of the burn.

Cooling should be done using clean, cool water—but not too cold(minimum 8 degree celcius), as excessively cold water increases the risk of hypothermia and peripheral vasoconstriction, which can worsen tissue perfusion and may contribute to converting the potentially salvageable zone of stasis into the irreversible zone of coagulation. For the same reason, cooling should not exceed 20 minutes.

### Secondary Survey

After initial stabilization, a focused and thorough examination is performed to identify all injuries and begin definitive care. The secondary survey consists of:

* **AMPLE history**: Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, Events leading to injury
* **Full head-to-toe examination**
* **Re-evaluation of the burn wound extent and depth**
* **Identification of associated trauma** (e.g., fractures, blunt injuries)
* **Radiologic imaging if indicated**
* **Monitoring of vital signs, urine output, and repeated clinical assessment**
* **Tetanus prophylaxis**
* **Trombosis prophylaxis**
* **Consider referral to a burn center**

This structured approach ensures that no injuries are missed and guides ongoing treatment and transfer decisions.

# Inhalation Injury and Toxic Gas Exposure

Inhalation injury is a serious complication of burn trauma, particularly in patients exposed to smoke in enclosed spaces. The injury consists of both a local thermal and chemical insult to the airway, as well as systemic toxicity from inhaled gases such as carbon monoxide and hydrogen cyanide.

## Local Airway Injury

Thermal and chemical damage to the upper airways (nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx) can cause airway edema and obstruction.

* Look for signs such as facial burns, singed nasal hairs, hoarseness, stridor, and carbonaceous sputum
* Early airway management is critical — consider early intubation
* Bronchoscopy can help assess lower airway involvement

## Systemic Toxicity – Carbon Monoxide and Cyanide

Burns in enclosed spaces (e.g. house fires) increase the risk of inhalation injury and exposure to toxic gases such as carbon monoxide and hydrogen cyanide. Patients with soot in the mouth or nose, altered mental status, or cardiovascular instability may require empirical treatment.

### **Carbon Monoxide Poisoning**

* + Treat with **100% oxygen** via non-rebreather mask or intubation with high FiO2
 + Monitor **carboxyhemoglobin** levels via blood gas analysis
 + Hyperbaric oxygen therapy may be considered in severe cases

### **Cyanide Poisoning**

* + Suspected in patients with lactic acidosis, hypotension, or coma after smoke inhalation—especially if the fire occurred in enclosed spaces containing synthetic materials (e.g., furniture, mattresses), which can release hydrogen cyanide when burned
 + **Cyanokit (hydroxocobalamin)** is the first-line antidote:
 - Binds cyanide to form cyanocobalamin (vitamin B12), which is excreted renally

# Assessing Burn Depth

Burn depth is a key factor in determining appropriate treatment, healing potential, and the need for surgical intervention.

Burn wounds are classified based on how deep into the skin the thermal injury extends:

* **Epidermal burns (1st degree)**: Involve only the epidermis. The skin appears red and dry, and the burn is painful. No blisters are present. These burns heal spontaneously within 5–7 days without scarring.
* **Superficial dermal (superficial 2nd degree) burns**: Involve the epidermis and upper dermis. The skin is pale pink, moist, and blistered, and the burn is very painful. Present capillary refill. Healing typically occurs within 7–14 days with minimal scarring.
* **Deep dermal (deep 2nd degree) burns**: Extend into the deeper layers of the dermis. The wound may appear pale and less moist. Blisters may be present or ruptured. There is reduced pain due to nerve damage. reduced or absent capillary refill. These burns may take long to heal and often require surgical treatment such as skin grafting.
* **Full-thickness (3rd degree) burns**: Destroy both epidermis and entire dermis. The skin is dry, leathery, and may appear white, brown, or black. Little or no pain due to nerve destruction. Absent capillary refill Healing requires excision and skin grafting.
* **Fourth-degree burns**: Extend into subcutaneous fat, muscle, or bone. The area may appear charred and dry with exposed tissue. These require extensive surgical and critical care management.

Healing potential is dependent on the presence of viable skin appendages (e.g., hair follicles, sweat glands) which can serve as reservoirs for epithelial regeneration. The deeper the burn, the fewer these structures remain. In full-thickness burns, re-epithelialization from the wound base is not possible, and healing must occur from the wound edges.

Skin thickness varies between different anatomical regions. Children and elderly individuals have thinner skin, making them more susceptible to deeper burns from the same thermal exposure.

It is essential to perform cleaning and removal of blisters before assessment.

A qualified clinical evaluation of burn depth is challenging for several reasons. Burn wounds are often heterogeneous in depth across the affected area, and their appearance may evolve during the first few days due to progression of tissue injury. This dynamic development can make depth estimation difficult in the early phase. Accurate evaluation requires experience and repeated assessment. If available, Laser Doppler Imaging (LDI) may support the evaluation and serve as a useful indicator of whether a burn is likely to heal spontaneously. However, it can only be used reliably within the 48–72 hour window after injury, when tissue perfusion is clearly defined.

# Estimating Burn Size – TBSA

Accurate assessment of the Total Body Surface Area (TBSA) burned is essential for guiding resuscitation and referral. Only burns deeper than epidermal (1st degree) are included—i.e., from superficial dermal (superficial 2nd degree) and deeper.

## **Methods of Estimation:**

### **Rule of Nines**

#### **Adults and children from 9 years old**

This is a fast and easy method to estimate the percentage of the body surface area affected by burns. It allows clinicians to quickly form an overview of the extent of injury and guide initial management. The body is divided into anatomical regions, each corresponding to approximately 9% (or a multiple) of the total body surface area (TBSA).-

* + Head and neck: 9%
 + Each arm: 9%
 + Each leg: 18%
 + Front of torso: 18%
 + Back of torso: 18%
 + Perineum: 1%

#### Children under 9 years old

Due to the relatively larger head and smaller legs in infants and younger children, a modified distribution is used. For a newborn (0 years):

* + Head: 18%
 + Each arm: 9%
 + Each leg: 14%
 + Front of torso: 18%
 + Back of torso: 18%

For each year of age until 9 years old, 1% is subtracted from the head and distributed as +0.5% to each leg.

For example:

* + A **1-year-old** child has:
 - Head: 17% (18 - 1)
 - Each leg: 14.5% (14 + 0.5)
 + A **6-year-old** child has:
 - Head: 12% (18 - 6)
 - Each leg: 17% (14 + 3.0)

From 9 years of age, the adult calculations apply, including 1% TBSA being allocated to the perineum.

### **Palmar Method**

The Rule of Nines is a rapid and widely used method for estimating burn size but may be less suitable for scattered or irregularly shaped burns. In these cases, the Palmar Method is useful:

* + The patient’s own palm, including fingers, is roughly equal to 1% of their total body surface area (TBSA). This makes it a simple and practical tool for estimating the size of smaller or scattered burns. It is especially helpful when only part of a body region is affected, as it allows for more precise adjustments—adding or subtracting area estimates when the full region used in the Rule of Nines is not involved

# Fluid Resuscitation in Burns

Fluid resuscitation is a cornerstone of initial burn care and is essential for restoring circulating volume, ensuring perfusion of vital organs, and preventing burn shock.

Larger burn injuries—typically those involving more than 20% TBSA in adults or 10% in children—can result in a systemic inflammatory response. This leads to widespread capillary leakage, causing fluid loss into the interstitial space (edema) and significant intravascular hypovolemia. Timely fluid replacement is crucial to avoid this.

The aim of fluid therapy is not to prevent edema but to support circulation and oxygen delivery. Over-resuscitation should be avoided, as it may cause exacerbate swelling and electrolyte disturbances. Fluid requirements should be calculated using the modified Parkland formula and continuously adjusted based on urinary output and clinical signs.

If a patient is hypovolemic shortly after trauma, clinicians should consider whether another cause of hypovolemia, such as bleeding, is present. ATLS principles should guide the initial trauma assessment.

## **When to Start Fluid Resuscitation**

* Adults and children ≥15 years: Burns involving ≥20% TBSA
* Children <15 years: Burns involving ≥10% TBSA

Dehydrated patients (e.g., elderly) with smaller TBSA involvement but low urine output may still need fluid supplementation.

## Modified Parkland Formula

The modified Parkland formula is used to calculate fluid requirements during the first 24 hours after a burn injury:

**3 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA burned** (minus any fluids already administered prior to arrival)

Half of the calculated volume is administered in the first 8 hours from the time of injury (not from admission). The remaining half is given over the next 16 hours.

This formula is an estimate only and should always be adjusted based on the patient's clinical response.

**Example:** A 70 kg adult with 30% TBSA. The total fluid requirement is:

3 mL × 70 kg × 30% = 6300 mL

The patient received 500 mL during ambulance transport.

**Remaining volume to administer = 6300 mL – 500 mL = 5800 mL**

* First half: 2900 mL
* Second half: 2900 mL

**Important:** Fluid calculation starts at the **time of injury**, not upon hospital arrival.

For example, if the patient arrives 2 hours after injury:

* Only 6 hours remain of the first 8-hour period
* The 2900 mL from the first half must be given over 6 hours
* **Rate = 2900 ÷ 6 ≈ 483 mL/hour**

The second half (2900 mL) is administered over the next 16-hour period (from 8 to 24 hours post-injury).

**Rate = 2900 ÷ 16 ≈ 181 mL/hour**

# Wound Management in Burns

Proper wound care is essential for pain relief, infection control, and optimal healing in burn patients. The process should follow a structured approach:

1. Debridement of blisters and loose skin
All blisters must be carefully clipped, and any loose or devitalized skin should be removed. This step is necessary to allow proper wound assessment, promote epithelialization, and reduce the risk of infection.

2. Wound cleansing
The wound should be washed thoroughly with water and mild soap. In cases of major burns, the entire body—including the hair—should be washed to reduce the risk of infection. If the burn involves the face or scalp, it is important to shave the hair in affected areas to prevent contamination and reduce infection risk.

## 3. Dressing technique

* A non-adherent wound contact layer should be applied directly to the burn wound. This layer should be cut to fit the size and shape of the burn area only. Common options include **Mepilex Transfer** or **Jelonet** (two layers of vaseline-impregnated gauze; only to be used if Mepilex is not available).
* On top of the contact layer, a thick layer of absorbent gauze is placed to manage the significant exudate often produced by burn wounds.
* Compression is then applied:
 + **Extremities:** Use elastic bandages to reduce edema and secure the dressing. The compressive gauze and bandages may extend beyond the wound onto healthy skin.
 + **Torso/Thorax:** Use an elastic net shirt to hold the dressings in place

## 4. Special areas

* **Face:** Apply an **Epicite hydrogel mask** for moisture and protection, followed by two layers of Jelonet. Place a layer of absorbent gauze over the Jelonet, and secure the dressing with a net cap to keep it in place
* **Hands:** Fingers should be individually wrapped to preserve mobility and avoid stiffness. Maintaining maximal range of motion is essential. On day 3, the outer dressing (absorbent gauze and compression) can be changed to a lighter version to improve hand function, as exudation typically decreases by then.
* **Perineum and other non-dressable areas:** Use Vaseline ointment liberally to protect the area.

Applying an appropriate wound contact layer reduces pain and risk of infection.

## 5. Dressing duration and surgical considerations

In deep burns, early surgery may be required to remove necrotic tissue and reduce infection risk. However, in superficial to mid-dermal burns where there is a chance of spontaneous healing, the dressing should ideally remain in place for 10–14 days to allow the wound to re-epithelialize undisturbed.

If necessary, the outer layers of the dressing (absorbent gauze and compression) can be changed without disturbing the inner wound contact layer. Removing the inner layer prematurely risks damaging the fragile new epithelium.

If infection is suspected, the entire dressing must be removed to allow inspection and appropriate wound treatment.

## 6. Dressing removal and follow-up

On day 10–14, dressing removal can usually be performed by the patient’s general practitioner or local hospital. If areas of deep dermal burns are identified that have not healed spontaneously, the patient should be referred to a specialized burn center for further surgical evaluation and treatment.

The goal is to avoid premature dressing changes that may disrupt healing, while still allowing sufficient follow-up to identify delayed healing or complications in time.

# Special Burn Types: Electrical and Chemical Burns

Certain types of burns require specific approaches due to their unique pathophysiology and risks. Two important subtypes are electrical burns and chemical burns.

## **Electrical Burns**

Electrical injuries may appear minor on the surface but can cause extensive internal damage. The severity depends on voltage, duration of contact, current path, and tissue resistance.

When electrical energy enters the body, it is converted into heat as it moves through tissues. Tissues with high electrical resistance, such as bone and tendons, generate significant thermal energy. Even after current ceases, bone temperature may remain elevated, prolonging heat damage to adjacent structures like periosteum, muscle, blood vessels, and nerves. These deep injuries can result in compartment syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, haemochromogenuria, osteonecrosis, and injuries to internal organs including the central nervous system, heart, lungs, and gastrointestinal tract.

The extent of internal damage is often disproportionate to the external appearance of cutaneous burns. Therefore, thorough assessment and vigilant monitoring are essential.

### **Types of electrical burns:**

* **Low-voltage injuries (under 1000 volts):** Usually domestic or workplace-related. These injuries typically cause localized full-thickness contact burns but rarely result in systemic effects. Standard wound care is usually sufficient. Cardiac monitoring is only required if there are electrocardiographic abnormalities or cardiac symptoms.
* **High-voltage injuries (over 1000 volts):** These may result in full-thickness contact burns and extensive deep tissue injury. Two primary injury mechanisms include:
 + **Current passing through the body:** Can cause profound deep tissue injury and internal organ damage, often disproportionate to the visible cutaneous burns.

Internal injuries may involve muscle, bone, and neurovascular structures, leading to severe complications.

* + - Aggressive fluid resuscitation is essential regardless of the size of the external burns and a urinary catheterization is necessary to monitor the output. Because of the risk of myoglobinuria and potential kidney failure, the urine output target should be higher than in standard fluid resuscitation: 1 mL/kg/h in adults and 1–2 mL/kg/h in children
 - When the injury cause damage from within, deep muscle layers may be non-viable even if outer layer of muscle looks intact. Early fasciotomy may be required to relieve pressure and prevent further tissue loss.
 - ECG monitoring is required.
 - Cutaneous wounds should be managed as for any thermal burn.
 + **Flash burns from electrical arcs:** These may cause extensive superficial burns to the skin surface without current transmission through the body. Clothing ignition can further increase the affected surface area.
 - Flash burns should be treated as thermal burns and do not carry the same risk for internal injury as direct current injuries.
 + Keep in mind that a high-voltage injury may involve both mechanisms - flash burns and deep tissue damage caused by electrical current passing through the body.
* **Lightning injuries:** Extremely high-voltage but very short duration. Can result in superficial burns, cardiac arrest, tympanic membrane rupture, and neurological complications such as delayed brain atrophy. Lichtenberg figures may be observed.

**Key considerations:**

* Identify  **entry and exit wounds**.
* Perform **ECG monitoring** when indicated.
* Initiate **urinary catheterization** in all high-voltage injuries to monitor urine output and assess for rhabdomyolysis.
* Begin **fluid resuscitation** early. Adjust fluids to maintain target urine output.
* Monitor extremities for signs of swelling and ischemia. Perform **fasciotomy** promptly if compartment syndrome is suspected.
* Conduct a full **secondary survey** to identify additional trauma or organ dysfunction.
* Repeat **neurological evaluations** regularly due to risk of delayed complications.

## **Chemical Burns**

Chemical burns result from exposure to corrosive substances like acids, alkalis, or organic compounds. The damage occurs through protein denaturation and cellular destruction. While symptoms can resemble those of thermal burns, chemical burns require immediate dilution rather than cooling.

### **Mechanism and classification:**

* **Acids (pH < 7):** Cause coagulative necrosis and form a protective eschar, which may limit depth of injury.
* **Alkalis (pH > 7):** Cause liquefactive necrosis and penetrate deeper into tissue, resulting in more extensive damage.

### **Initial approach and treatment:**

* Remove clothing and brush off any powder agents.
* Irrigate immediately—ideally within seconds. Use large volumes of water for at least 30 minutes (or longer in case of strong alkali exposure). Ensure the runoff flows away from unaffected skin.
* Use Diphoterine or Hexaflurine if available. Diphoterine neutralizes both acids and bases and can be used on skin, eyes, and mucosa.
* Consider pain relief and monitor for hypothermia during prolonged rinsing.
* Measure wound pH using litmus paper if available.

### **Special agents and additional measures:**

* **Hydrofluoric acid:** Requires urgent treatment with topical calcium gluconate gel or local injection. Hypocalcemia may lead to arrhythmias and systemic toxicity even from small surface exposures.
* **Phenol:** Highly toxic and anesthetic—penetrates deeply. Requires decontamination and monitoring for systemic toxicity.
* **Cement:** High-pH caustic agent. Injuries may appear late. Irrigation should be continued for extended periods.
* **Petroleum products (e.g., gasoline):** May cause systemic toxicity and deep tissue damage, even without ignition.
* **Phosphorus:** Ignites in air and continues burning. Requires immersion in water and manual removal of particles.

### **Anamnesis should include:**

* Chemical identity (substance, form, concentration, and pH if known)
* Mode, duration, and extent of exposure

### **Additional considerations:**

* **Inhalation injuries** from fumes should prompt early airway assessment.
* **Ocular exposure** is an emergency—initiate continuous eye irrigation and consult ophthalmology.
* **Referral to burn center** is recommended for facial, ocular, perineal involvement, large TBSA, or systemic symptoms.
* **Early irrigation is key** to limiting damage—ideally begun at the site of injury and continued during transport.
* **Assessment of burn depth and need for fluid resuscitation** should be done based on extent and clinical presentation.
* **Monitor for systemic complications** like electrolyte imbalance, especially in exposure to hydrofluoric acid or phenol.

Patients with uncertain depth or evolving wounds should be reassessed regularly. Suspected deep burns, ongoing pain, or systemic symptoms require evaluation at a specialized burn unit.

# **Hypothermia:**

A decrease in tissue temperature can occur locally, systemically, or as a combination of both. Hypothermia encompasses a range of conditions from mild peripheral cooling to profound systemic core temperature reduction, and should be assessed accordingly. It can be life-threatening, leading to impaired consciousness, cardiac arrhythmias, coagulopathy, and multi-organ dysfunction if not recognized and managed promptly.

## **Systemic hypothermia**

Systemic hypothermia is defined as a core body temperature below 35°C. It must be treated first to restore adequate circulation and prevent further tissue damage before managing peripheral frostbite. Treatment includes passive, active external, and active internal rewarming strategies:

* **Passive external rewarming**: Warm environment, blankets, dry clothing, head covering.
* **Active external rewarming**: Warm forced air blankets, warmed intravenous fluids.
* **Active internal rewarming**: Warmed humidified oxygen, gastric, peritoneal, pleural, or bladder lavage with 40°C isotonic saline.

## Frostbite

Frostbite is a local cold-induced injury in which tissue freezing causes both cellular and vascular damage. It most often affects the extremities such as fingers, toes, nose, ears, and cheeks. The severity of frostbite ranges from mild, reversible tissue injury to deep, irreversible necrosis that may require surgical intervention or amputation.

### Mechanism and Pathophysiology

Frostbite occurs when tissue temperature drops below 0°C, resulting in the formation of ice crystals within and around cells. This leads to:

* Mechanical disruption of cells
* Microvascular occlusion
* Ischemia and hypoxia
* Inflammatory mediator release

Both direct freezing damage and reperfusion injury contribute to tissue destruction. Rapid cooling tends to cause more severe intracellular ice crystal formation, leading to dehydration and cell death. Reperfusion exacerbates tissue injury via endothelial damage, fibrin deposition, and microthrombi formation, resulting in ischemia.

### Classification

Frostbite is typically classified into four degrees:

* **First-degree**: Superficial injury with erythema and edema; no blisters; skin remains soft.
* **Second-degree**: Superficial dermal injury with clear fluid-filled blisters.
* **Third-degree**: Deep dermal injury with hemorrhagic blisters and skin necrosis.
* **Fourth-degree**: Full-thickness involvement including muscles, tendons, and bones; dry, black eschar may form.

Severity and risk of amputation may also be assessed using imaging modalities such as angiography, bone scans, or MRI.

### Treatment Algorithm for Frostbite

#### Step 1: Initial Management

* **Rewarming in water bath with chlorhexidine** (37–40°C with 100 mL 5% chlorhexidine in 1 L NaCl), for 30–60 minutes
* **Ibuprofen 800 mg** orally
* **Tetanus prophylaxis**, if indicated
* **Blood samples**: Hb, electrolytes, LPK, CRP, myoglobin, CK, coagulation factors
* **Clinical assessment** of tissue viability after rewarming
* **Botulinum toxin A injections** around peripheral vessels (fingers/toes) to limit vasoconstriction, if indicated

#### Step 2: Classification

* **Superficial frostbite (grade 1–2)**:
 + Analgesia: Paracetamol and possibly opioids
 + Remove clear fluid blisters
 + Dress with Mepilex or alternatively two layers of Jelonet and absorbent gauze
 + Elevate affected extremities
 + Ibuprofen 400 mg orally twice daily
* **Deep dermal frostbite (grade 3–4)**:
 + If cyanosis or weak capillary response distal to PIP joint or weak peripheral pulse on Doppler:
 - **Imaging**: Angiography or SPECT/CT if not contraindicated
 - If reduced distal perfusion:
 * If **no contraindication** and within 24 hours of rewarming: initiate **thrombolysis with alteplase (tPA)** for max 12 hours, **concurrent heparin infusion**, and repeat angiography daily until normalization or 48 hours without improvement
 * If **contraindication** to thrombolysis: initiate **vasodilation with Iloprost** and repeat angiography daily until perfusion is normalized or after 48 hours without improvement
 - If no signs of impaired perfusion: treat as superficial frostbite

#### Step 3: Aftercare

* Local wound care
* Ibuprofen 400 mg orally twice daily for 4 weeks
* Paracetamol +/- opioids as needed
* Vitamin C 1 g orally daily for 6 weeks
* Acetylsalicylic acid 75 mg orally daily for 6 weeks
* Consider regional nerve block
* Hyperbaric oxygen therapy if indicated

#### Surgical Treatment

* Definitive surgery is delayed until clear tissue demarcation, usually weeks to months
* Acute fasciotomy may be necessary for compartment syndrome
* Early amputation may be indicated in case of sepsis or progressive liquefactive necrosis
